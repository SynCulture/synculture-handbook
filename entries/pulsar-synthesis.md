## Overview

Pulsar synthesis generates electronic sound from trains of sonic particles called *pulsarets*. Each pulsaret is a brief waveform, typically lasting between a tenth of a millisecond and around ten milliseconds, shaped by an amplitude envelope. These particles are emitted in periodic or stochastic trains. When the train rate enters the audio frequency range (above roughly 20 Hz), the result is a pitched tone whose timbre is determined by the waveform and envelope of the individual pulsaret. Below audio rate, the train is perceived as a rhythm.

The name is borrowed from astronomy. An astrophysical pulsar is a rapidly rotating neutron star whose beamed radio emission sweeps past the Earth once per rotation, arriving as a periodic signal. The slowest known pulsars turn about once every four seconds, the fastest at over seven hundred rotations per second, so the emission spans roughly 0.25 Hz to 700 Hz (Pietruszewski 2022). That range crosses the boundary between heard pulse and heard tone, which is the boundary the fundamental frequency parameter traverses. Pulsars were discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967 (Hewish et al. 1968).

The technique was proposed by Curtis Roads in the late 1990s and formalised in *Microsound* (2001). It belongs to the broader family of particle-based synthesis methods that descend from Dennis Gabor's {% xref "acoustical-quanta", "acoustical quanta" %} (1946, 1947) and Iannis Xenakis's work on {% xref "granular-synthesis", "granular synthesis" %}. Roads took the borrowing from astronomy literally enough to include, among his typical pulsaret waveforms, a "cosmic pulsar waveform emitted by the neutron star Vela X-1" (Roads 2001a, fig. 4.11e), so a rotation profile enters the technique as a table like any other.

Pulsar synthesis differs from granular synthesis in several respects. Where granular synthesis typically works with stored sound samples or sustained grains of synthetic waveforms, pulsar synthesis operates with electronically generated particles whose waveform and envelope are independently variable parameters. The separation of *pulsaret waveform* (which determines spectral content) from *pulsaret envelope* (which determines bandwidth and formant structure, the resonant peaks that give a sound its vocal or instrumental character) gives the technique a distinctive parametric architecture.

## Genealogy

Roads describes pulsar synthesis as a method that "melds established principles within a new paradigm" (Roads 2001a). Its lineage runs along three distinct threads: the filtered pulse trains of analog electronic music, the particle-based formant synthesis techniques of the 1970s and 1980s, and the microsound paradigm descending from Gabor.

**Filtered pulse trains (1950s-1960s).** In its basic form, pulsar synthesis "generates electronic pulses and pitched tones similar to those produced by analog instruments such as the Ondioline (Jenny 1958) and the Hohner Elektronium (introduced in 1950), which were designed around the principle of filtered pulse trains" (Roads 2001a). The same principle was central to studio practice in the {% xref "elektronische-musik", "elektronische Musik" %} of the Cologne studio: Karlheinz Stockhausen and Gottfried Michael Koenig used filtered impulse generation as a staple of their craft. Stockhausen's theoretical essay *. . . wie die Zeit vergeht . . .* (1957) articulated the continuity between rhythm and pitch as a single temporal continuum traversed by acceleration. That is the perceptual territory pulsar synthesis makes directly playable through its fundamental frequency parameter.

**Particle-based formant synthesis (1970s-1980s).** A pulsaret with a sharp attack and exponential decay reproduces "a well-known configuration for formant synthesis, as seen in techniques such as window function synthesis, VOSIM (Kaegi and Tempelaars 1978), and FOF (Rodet 1984)" (Roads 2001a). Roads positions these {% xref "formant-synthesis", "formant synthesis" %} techniques as special cases of pulsar synthesis: where FOF fixes the waveform (a sine) and constrains the envelope, pulsar synthesis leaves both freely variable.

**Microsound (1946-2001).** Gabor proposed that any sound can be decomposed into elementary {% xref "acoustical-quanta", "acoustical quanta" %}: short time-frequency atoms, finite in both time and frequency, occupying a cell in the joint time-frequency plane whose minimum area is bounded by the uncertainty principle (Gabor 1946, 1947). Iannis Xenakis gave the particle paradigm its first compositional framework with {% xref "granular-synthesis", "granular synthesis" %}. Pulsar synthesis "belongs to a larger family of microsonic or particle synthesis techniques" (Roads 2001a), but within this family its parentage is specific: it descends from the impulse and formant traditions above, not from the granulation of sampled sound.

**Roads's synthesis of these strands (1991-2001).** Roads developed pulsar synthesis from an initial implementation in James McCartney's Synth-O-Matic (1991), used to compose *Clang-Tint* (1994), through a SuperCollider 1 instrument coded with Stephen T. Pope in 1997 (Pope 1997), to the PulsarGenerator application built with Alberto de Campo in SuperCollider 2 (1999-2001). His contribution was to identify a parametric space these traditions had left unexplored: the independent control of pulsaret waveform, pulsaret envelope, and train characteristics (fundamental frequency, formant frequency, density), reconfigured as digital wavetables within the {% xref "microsound", "microsound" %} framework. As a digital technique it "accrues the advantages of precise programmable control, waveform flexibility, graphical interface, and extensibility" (Roads 2001a). The model predates its publication: the earliest surviving scripts are from 1997, and Roads points to conversations with Aldo Piccialli and colleagues in the Physics Department of the Università di Napoli "Federico II", and to three texts from that circle, as a source of inspiration (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 38, n. 7).

The following SC2 code is the original basic pulsar synthesis instrument created by Curtis Roads with Stephen T. Pope (1997). It declares wavetables for the pulsaret waveform, amplitude, fundamental frequency, formant frequency, and spatial position. Each parameter is read from a control table via `Ktransient`, and `Apulse` generates the pulsarets. The binary `.Synth` file also contains the wavetable data itself (waveform, envelopes, control trajectories) as embedded float arrays.

<figure class="code-historical">

```lua
-- P U L S A R  S Y N T H E S I S
-- Copyright 1997 Curtis Roads

-- A basic pulsar generator, with envelopes for:
--  overall amplitude of a pulsar train
--  fundamental frequency
--  formant frequency
--  spatial envelope
-- The pulsaret waveform is arbitrary and can be changed.

-- Declare the outputs
defaudioout L, R;

-- Declare the wave tables and envelopes
deftable wave, ampl, fundamental, formant, spatial_position;

start {

    -- Declare local variables
    var pulsar, a_env, fun_env, form_env, pos_env, dur;

    dur = 10.0;

    a_env = Ktransient(ampl, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    fun_env = Ktransient(fundamental, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    form_env = Ktransient(formant, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    pos_env = Ktransient(spatial_position, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);

    pulsar = Apulse(wave, 0, 0);

    {   (pulsar.value(fun_env.value, form_env.value) *! a_env.value)
                .pan2out(pos_env.value, L, R);
    }.dspAdd;
}
```

The <code>Apulse</code> UGen reads an arbitrary pulsaret waveform and is controlled by wavetable-driven envelopes for fundamental frequency, formant frequency, amplitude, and spatial position. </figure>

**The pulsaret** is related to but distinct from several precursors: the {% xref "elektronische-musik", "Klangbaustein" %} (sound building block) concept in early German electronic music; the *grain* in Xenakis and Roads's granular synthesis; and the *wavelet* in mathematical signal analysis. Roads explicitly positions the pulsaret as a particle that is neither a grain (too long, too tied to stored samples) nor a sample (too short, no envelope shaping). In advanced practice, both the waveform and envelope tables can be loaded from a sound sample; the pulsaret waveform and envelope remain architecturally distinct, but the resulting pulsaret begins to resemble the windowed grain of granular synthesis, and the boundary between the two techniques blurs. What to call the micro-element is itself a substantive question rather than a matter of taste. {% xref "acoustical-quanta", "Atom" %}, {% xref "granular-synthesis", "grain" %}, {% xref "acoustical-quanta", "quantum" %}, {% xref "unit-generator", "unit" %}, {% xref "sampling", "sample" %}, {% xref "dictionary-synthesis", "dictionary entry" %} and {% xref "event", "event" %} each carry a different lineage, and each commits the work that uses it to different conceptual operations.

## Commitments

Pulsar synthesis sits apart from most other synthesis techniques. It emerged from the {% xref "microsound", "microsound" %} paradigm, which sought to theorise electronic sound at temporal scales below the note and above the sample, and that tradition is itself marked by the disciplinary crossings SYNCULTURE investigates: mathematics (quantum theory, stochastic processes), engineering (signal processing, filter design), and musical composition (electroacoustic and computer music).

Four features follow from that inheritance. They are set out here, before the mechanism, because each is a commitment rather than a feature in the ordinary sense, and each is audible in the instrument below. Together they are what make the technique a productive case study for the analysis of synthesis as a *cultural technique*.

**The particle as compositional primitive.** Pulsar synthesis foregrounds the particle rather than the waveform, the note, or the sample as the basic unit of musical material. This represents a specific epistemological commitment: that sound is best understood and manipulated not as a continuous wave but as a stream of discrete events. The commitment has roots in Gabor's information theory, but its musical interpretation is not determined by the physics. Composers have made very different aesthetic choices within it.

**The duty cycle as timbral continuum.** The duty cycle parameter exposes a continuous transition between pitched tone and broadband noise within a single synthesis mechanism. In classical synthesis, these are typically separate domains (oscillators vs noise generators). Pulsar synthesis dissolves this boundary, suggesting that pitch and noise are endpoints of a single parametric axis rather than categorically different phenomena.

**Formant structure without physical models.** The decoupled formant frequency parameter produces spectral resonances reminiscent of vocal or instrumental formants without any reference to a physical source. This is a form of {% xref "nonstandard-synthesis", "nonstandard synthesis" %} in the sense defined by Holtzman (1978): the sound is specified algorithmically rather than by modelling a physical system. The resulting timbres evoke vocal qualities while remaining irreducibly synthetic.

**Micro-macro temporal bridging.** Because the fundamental frequency parameter can span from sub-audio rhythmic rates to audio-frequency pitches, pulsar synthesis makes audible the continuity between rhythm and pitch that is central to the microsound thesis. A single parameter sweep can traverse the perceptual boundary between discrete pulses and fused tone, demonstrating what Roads calls the {% xref "time-scales", "time scale continuum" %}.

## How it works

Roads gives the formal definition in *Microsound*. "A single pulsar is a particle of sound. It consists of an arbitrary pulsaret waveform *w* with a period *d* followed by a silent time interval *s*" (Roads 2001a, 138). The two intervals together fix the period of the train:

<div class="formal-definition">
  <p class="formula"><em>p</em> = <em>d</em> + <em>s</em></p>
  <p>"where <em>p</em> is the pulsar period, <em>d</em> is the duty cycle, and <em>s</em> is silent" (Roads 2001a, 138).</p>
  <p class="formula"><em>f<sub>p</sub></em> = 1/<em>p</em> &nbsp;&nbsp; <em>f<sub>d</sub></em> = 1/<em>d</em></p>
  <p>"Let us define the frequency corresponding to the repetition period as <em>f<sub>p</sub></em> = 1/<em>p</em> and the frequency corresponding to the duty cycle as <em>f<sub>d</sub></em> = 1/<em>d</em>. Typical ranges of <em>f<sub>p</sub></em> are between 1 Hz and 5 kHz, the typical range of <em>f<sub>d</sub></em> is from 80 Hz to 10 kHz" (Roads 2001a, 138).</p>
</div>

Two consequences follow, and both are audible in the instrument below. Because *p* is fixed by *d* + *s*, "lowering the fundamental means increasing *s*, and raising the fundamental means decreasing *s*" (Roads 2001a, 140). And because *f<sub>p</sub>* and *f<sub>d</sub>* are separately controllable, the rate of emission and the spectral peak move independently: this decoupling is what gives pulsar tones their {% xref "formant-synthesis", "formant" %} structure. In the cells below *f<sub>p</sub>* is the argument `fund` and *f<sub>d</sub>* is `form`, which sets the particle's duration as `form.reciprocal`, that is *d*.

A pulsaret is the product of two lookup tables: a *pulsaret waveform* table and a *pulsaret envelope* table. In the original Pulsar Generator program by Roads and de Campo, each is a {% xref "wavetable-synthesis", "wavetable" %} of 2048 floating-point values that can hold any arbitrary shape. The synthesiser reads through both in parallel and multiplies them sample by sample. The waveform determines the spectral content of the particle; the envelope shapes its amplitude over time. Their product is the pulsaret, the elementary sonic particle emitted at each period of the train.

The choice of envelope determines the spectral bandwidth of the pulsaret: a narrow envelope such as a Gaussian or exponential decay concentrates energy around the formant frequency and produces a sharp resonant peak, while a wide envelope approaching the full period yields a nearly pure tone. The choice of waveform determines the fine spectral structure within that bandwidth: a sine produces a single formant peak, a sawtooth fills in the harmonics, and a noise waveform distributes energy stochastically. Because the two tables are multiplied sample by sample, every combination produces a distinct spectral profile. The catalogues above show standard shapes, but in practice both tables can hold any arbitrary curve, hand-drawn or algorithmically generated.

As the duty cycle shrinks relative to the period, more silence enters the signal and the spectrum broadens. At the limit, an infinitely narrow pulsaret gives a click train with a flat spectrum; at the other extreme, an envelope that fills the whole period leaves no silence at all and the result approaches a conventional oscillator.

Fundamental and formant frequency are decoupled, which permits effects analogous to the {% xref "vocoder", "source-filter model" %} of speech: pitch varies independently of resonant structure. Train density is the odd one out in this table. The Pulsar Generator implements it as pulsar masking and nuPG as a pulse probability mask, but the browser instrument below has no control for it, so in the cells that follow every period carries a pulsaret. The downloadable file at the end of the entry does have one, in the two forms the PG implements for a single train, burst and stochastic.

Nothing sounds yet. A `SynthDef` is a description of a signal graph, compiled

What the definition says, in order. `Impulse.ar` emits a single-sample click
`fund` times a second, which is the train of triggers. Each trigger starts one
pulsaret. `GrainBuf` makes that pulsaret by reading the waveform table once
through, multiplying it by the envelope table as it goes, and placing the result
in the stereo field. The particle lasts `1/form` seconds, so raising the formant
shortens the particle and changes nothing else. `EnvGen` holds the whole thing
open while the gate is 1 and lets it go when the gate closes. The rest is
arithmetic tying those parts together.

For readers who already write SuperCollider, the rate calculation is the line
worth pausing on. `rate = form * BufDur.kr(wavebuf)` sets playback speed so that
the table is traversed exactly once per grain whatever the grain's length:
`BufDur` is the table's duration at unity rate, and multiplying by the formant
frequency makes the read complete in `1/form` seconds. `interp: 4` is cubic

these controls over OSC, and unsmoothed jumps in `fund` and `form` are audible

## Control by table

The instrument above is played the way instruments are usually played: a hand
moves a control, the sound follows. That is not how pulsar synthesis was
conceived. De Campo and Pietruszewski (2025) put the point plainly: pulsar
synthesis "was designed by Curtis Roads in conjunction with a special control
model: a set of tables which can be edited by drawing is used for designing both
waveforms (for pulsaret and envelope) and a group of control functions for
synthesis parameters over a given time."

Two claims are folded together there: that the table is the object of
composition, and that the loop is the process that makes it sound. The section
after this one takes both apart. What matters here is that the cells below are
the first place either becomes audible.

The cell below follows figure 16.16 of de Campo and Pietruszewski (2025) and the
closing step of their figure 16.17. It
generates four control tables and creates a synth that reads them.
`BufDur.kr(bufnum) / fundtime` sets the traversal so a table is read once
per its own number of seconds, and `PlayBuf` with `loop: 1` repeats it. Evaluate it a
second time while it sounds: the trajectories are rewritten under the
instrument, which never stops.

are the instrument.

How fast the tables are read, and in which direction, is a separate decision
from what they contain, and the definition takes it as a control rather than as
a property of the material. There is one value for it, the seconds a traversal
takes, and it is signed: `PlayBuf` with `loop: 1` reads backwards at a negative
rate and wraps at the start of the buffer, so -60 crosses a table in a minute
the other way without a value being rewritten. Each of the four tables has its
own, which is the more consequential move: given different times they stop being
one loop, each returning to its start after its own number of seconds, and they
coincide only where those periods do. Setting the four together puts them back
in step as well as on one period: the call resets the reading position with the
time, since a shared length between readings that started at different moments
is not one loop but four of equal size.

## The table and the loop

A table does nothing. It is an array of numbers held in memory, and it makes no
sound until something reads it. Pietruszewski (2024, p. 78) describes this
condition as potential rather than inert: the loop-form "functions at first as a
proposal, or to use Michael Koenig's term 'variant-form'. Whether as a graphic
shape or as a description of an algorithmic procedure, its existence is
potential. It exists 'outside of time', waiting to be hooked up to the actual
synthesis model and sound out." Xenakis states the underlying position flatly:
"music is outside of time and time serves only for it to manifest itself"
(Xenakis and Varga 1996, p. 83).

What converts the one state into the other is the reading. Wolfgang Ernst
supplies the term: "The elementary unit of a technological being-in-time is the
time-varying signal. When not in motion, a phonographically recorded acoustic
signal is not in its signal state but frozen in a state of graphic storage. It
becomes an operative media diagram only when turned back into a time object once
again, un-frozen and transduced by the movement of the apparatus and the sonic
pickup" (Ernst 2019, p. 684). In the Pulsar Generator the apparatus doing the
un-freezing is the emission clock. "Iterations over shapes as data structures
project structural into temporal order. Iteration counts and yields
outside-of-time structures in time" (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 79). The rate of that
counting is the pulsar emission rate itself, running "usually 1 to 3000, with
values between 35-60 functioning at the threshold of discrete pulses and a
continuous tone texture" (ibid.), and it is also the update clock: the program
reads every synthesis parameter once per iteration and not otherwise.

This is what makes a uniform table structure coherent rather than merely
economical, and it is the design decision the two programs below are built on:
tables of identical length, edited through identical graphic editors, spanning
the micro scale of the pulsaret and the meso scale of the train. "While
there is no visual difference between, for example, the 'pulsaret table' and
'fundamental frequency table', their role in the circuit differs significantly"
(Pietruszewski 2024, p. 79). The arrangement is object-oriented polymorphism:
"each of these loops represents the same basic unit, a sequence of floating
point values distributed horizontally along the x-axis... While its function
changes depending on the place in the synthesis circuit's logical structure, the
shape's basic form remains unchanged" (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 80).
The identity is not rhetorical. It licenses a shared vocabulary of operations,
"resampling to a new range, reversing, scaling, morphing with other values, and
erasing", which applies to a waveform and to a minute-long amplitude contour
alike, because both are the same kind of object until something reads them.
The point is pressed to its literal limit in an appendix that prints all 2048
values of a single table across fourteen pages (Pietruszewski 2024, pp. 139-152).

None of this begins with sound synthesis. The table is among the oldest objects
in computation, and it turns up in the nineteenth-century instruments that
precede electronic sound already doing this work. In the 1881 discussion of
Thomson's tide predictor before the Institution of Civil Engineers, the
Astronomer Royal George Biddell Airy described a table he had built with his
assistant Mr Ellis: all vulgar fractions with numerators and denominators not
exceeding 100, together with their logarithms, "arranged, not in the order of
numerators or in the order of denominators, but in the order of the ultimate
value of the fractions" (Thomson 1881, discussion, p. 40). It was for choosing
gear ratios. Given a required proportion between two tidal arguments, one took
its logarithm, ran an eye along the table, and found every fraction near that
value gathered in one place; the numbers "were taken at sight from the table".
The whole design is in the sort order, and the same values ordered by numerator
would have been useless. Computation ran on this trade for centuries, storage
against arithmetic, and it is the trade a wavetable oscillator still makes:
Mathews's stored function tables in MUSIC III and its successors exist because
computing a sine every sample was ruinous and reading one was not (Mathews
1969). A pulsaret table is a machine part of that kind. What distinguishes it is
that the rate of reading was made a musical parameter rather than fixed in the
gearing.

The predictor sits between tables. It reads one, Airy's fractions cut into the
teeth of its wheels, and it writes another, since what a tide-predicting machine
finally produces is a printed tide table. Both are in the same discussion. Mr Law
set prediction against record, comparing "the tables of the late Sir John
Lubbock" with the height the tide actually reached at the Shadwell entrance of
the London docks through January and February 1836, and where the two diverged he
named the residual by a third table, giving the force of the wind on that day
"according to the Admiralty table" (Thomson 1881, discussion, pp. 46-47). Three
tables doing three jobs: one read by a mechanism, one written for a reader, one
supplying a scale against which the unexplained could be measured. None of them
is simply a set of numbers. Each is a set of numbers together with a way of being
read, and the second half is where the work is done.

The cells above make the difference audible. `~setLoop.value(2)` and
`~setLoop.value(60)` traverse the same four tables at rates thirty times apart. At
two seconds the fundamental table is a gesture, a sweep across the boundary
between rhythm and pitch; at sixty the same curve is a slow drift, and what was a
gesture has become a form. None of the stored values changed. Only the reading
did. In these terms the loop "institutes a mediation between an inter-elementary,
macro-temporal whole and an intra-elementary micro-temporal fragment", where
"the focus is not on form as a fixed entity but on form as a transforming
modulator or a cascade of nested loops" (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 80).

Because the table and its reader are separate, the table can be rewritten while
the reading continues. This is "an essential generalisation within the
nuPG program... the ability to formalise a new loop at a run time of the
program" (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 81). In the Pulsar Generator it appears as a bank of
settings crossfaded at a variable rate, and in de Campo and Pietruszewski (2025,
fig. 16.18) as fresh tables sent to the buffers while the sound goes on. Here
`~newTables.value` replaces all four trajectories under an instrument that never
stops. This is not interrupting a sound in order to change it. It is
editing the memory the sound is reading from, which puts a composer in a
different relation to a running process than starting and stopping it does.

Four tables and a loop are already the whole control model. What the Pulsar
Generator and nuPG add, in the section that follows, is scale and an interface:
thirteen tables rather than four, drawn by hand rather than filled by code, with
the same 2048 values behind each one. Read in this order, their design reads
less as a set of engineering choices than as a position on where musical time
comes from.

## Instrumental implementations

### Pulsar Generator (PG)

The <a href="https://www.curtisroads.net/software">Pulsar Generator</a> was developed by Curtis Roads and Alberto de Campo in SuperCollider 2 at the University of California, Santa Barbara, and distributed by CREATE. It is the first instrumental encapsulation of the pulsar synthesis technique, providing wavetable-driven control over all core parameters: pulsar train duration, fundamental and formant frequency envelopes, pulsaret waveform and envelope, amplitude, and spatial path. The interface extends the basic synthesis engine with pulsar masking (burst, channel, and stochastic masking modes), convolution with sampled sounds, and multi-channel mixing (Fig. 08).

The program generates three simultaneous pulsar trains sharing one fundamental frequency table, each with independent formant, panning, and amplitude tables. This design dates back to Roads's first SuperCollider 1 sketches of 1997, where the configuration was called "additive": a unified clocking mechanism synchronises all three trains from a single fundamental frequency cycle, which around 2000 also kept processing costs low. The result is a conception of polyphony specific to pulsar synthesis: not multiple independent voices but multiple spectral-spatial interpretations of a single temporal stream (Pietruszewski 2024, 2026). The original SC1 script survives (Fig. 07): three `Apulse` generators read the same pulsaret waveform and are driven by one shared fundamental frequency envelope, while each follows its own formant and spatial position envelope.

<figure class="code-historical">

```lua
-- P U L S A R  S Y N T H E S I S
-- Copyright 1997 Curtis Roads

-- An additive pulsar generator. The pulsar streams share a common
-- fundamental frequency and waveform, but follow different formant
-- and spatial envelopes.

-- Declare the stereo output channels
defaudioout L, R;

-- Declare the wavetable and envelopes
deftable wave, ampl, fundamental, formant1, formant2, formant3,
    spatial_position1, spatial_position2, spatial_position3;

 -- Pulsar instrument
start {

    -- Declare local variables
    var osc, a_env, fun_env,
        form_env1, form_env2, form_env3,
        pos_env1, pos_env2, pos_env3,
        dur;

    dur = 30.0;

    -- Overall amplitude envelope
    a_env = Ktransient(ampl, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);

    -- Fundamental frequency envelope
    fun_env = Ktransient(fundamental, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);

    -- Formant envelopes
    form_env1 = Ktransient(formant1, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    form_env2 = Ktransient(formant2, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    form_env3 = Ktransient(formant3, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);

    pos_env1 = Ktransient(spatial_position1, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    pos_env2 = Ktransient(spatial_position2, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);
    pos_env3 = Ktransient(spatial_position3, dur, 1, 0, `dspRemove);

    osc1 = Apulse(wave, 0, 0);
    osc2 = Apulse(wave, 0, 0);
    osc3 = Apulse(wave, 0, 0);

    {  (osc1.value(fun_env.value, form_env1.value) *! a_env.value)
                .pan2out(pos_env1.value, L, R);
       (osc2.value(fun_env.value, form_env2.value) *! a_env.value)
                .pan2out(pos_env2.value, L, R);
       (osc3.value(fun_env.value, form_env3.value) *! a_env.value)
                .pan2out(pos_env3.value, L, R);

    }.dspAdd;

}
```

Every control object in the PG is a wavetable: thirteen tables in all, each stored as an array of 2048 floating-point values and presented through an identical graphic editor regardless of its temporal level of operation. The pulsaret waveform and envelope operate at the micro scale; the formant, panning, and amplitude tables shape the train over durations of seconds to a minute. The uniform table structure offers a single control model across time scales, mobilising a view of composition as creative grafting between temporal resolutions (Pietruszewski 2024). Roads calls this concept "graphical synthesis", and it connects the PG to earlier experiments in optical sound synthesis and to Xenakis's UPIC. Editing operations reinforce the cross-scale identity: de Campo implemented clipboard functions that mix or multiply the contents of one wavetable into another with automatic range scaling, so an amplitude envelope can be imposed directly onto a formant trajectory (Roads 2001c). Parameter states are stored as *settings* in a bank of sixteen slots, with variable-rate crossfading between them, a scheme that, as Roads notes, "takes performance with PulsarGenerator to another level of musical complexity" (Roads 2001a).

Masking is the PG's own contribution to the technique, and the one part of the
program that is not a way of drawing a parameter. A pulsar generator "emits a
metronomic sequence of pulsars"; masking "breaks up the stream by introducing
intermittancies (regular or irregular) into the metronomic sequence. It deletes
individual pulsarets, leaving an interval of silence in their place" (Roads
2001a, 149). Three kinds are implemented. *Burst masking* "models the burst
generators of the classic electronic music studios", alternating <em>b</em>
pulsarets with <em>r</em> silent periods, so a ratio of 4:2 gives
111100111100111100. *Channel masking* "selectively masks pulsars in two
channels, creating a dialog within a phrase by articulating each channel in
turn", generalisable to any number of channels. *Stochastic masking*
"introduces random intermittancy", implemented "as a weighted probability that
a pulsar will be emitted at a particular point in a pulsar train", drawn as an
envelope over the train: at 1 every pulsar sounds, at 0 none do, and Roads notes
that "values between 0.9 and 0.8 produce an interesting analog-like
intermittancy, as if there were an erratic contact in the synthesis circuit".

What makes masking more than a mute is that it changes what is heard rather than
how much. Below the rhythm threshold the effect is rhythmic, a figure cut out of
a steady pulse. Above it, burst masking imposes amplitude modulation on the
timbre and divides the fundamental by a subharmonic factor of <em>b</em> +
<em>r</em>, so a 2:1 ratio on a 100 Hz fundamental with a 400 Hz formant puts
subharmonics at 133 Hz and 266 Hz. Push the factor far enough and "the
subharmonic crosses through the threshold separating tone and rhythm", giving
"a series of alternating long tone pulses and silent intervals" (Roads 2001a,
149-51). Subtraction from the train turns into addition to the spectrum, which
is the clearest case in the whole technique of an operation meaning different
things at different time scales.

The implementation is compact: the entire program, including the graphical interface, required fewer than 1,500 lines of SuperCollider 2 code, with McCartney's real-time engine calculating the samples. Pulsar scheduling dominates the computational cost. At infrasonic emission rates the PG used under 4% of a 500 MHz PowerPC G4; a three-formant instrument at a 2 kHz fundamental, emitting six thousand pulsars per second, consumed roughly 45% (Roads 2001a).

The PG was thus shaped by the material conditions of computing circa 2000: the processing limits of Mac OS 9 and the buffer management strategies of that era. The 2048-point table and the quantization of parameter updates to the emission rate were pragmatic responses to these constraints, but they simultaneously encode a specific temporal ontology, musical time as discrete, recursive and quantized, in which the basic unit of temporal organization is not the continuous signal but the discrete event (Pietruszewski 2026). The design also embeds a compositional stance: the PG emphasises micro- and meso-temporal organisation and was conceived as a generator of sound material to be edited, transformed, and mixed in later stages, rather than a composer of finished macroform. Haworth (2015) reads the PG, alongside GENDYN, as a cultural object: a synthesis procedure functioning as a text within the discursive and aesthetic practices of electroacoustic music. Its recorded traces bear this out, from Marcus Schmickler's transformation of PG material on <em>Altars of Science</em> (2007) to Florian Hecker's presentation of raw, unprocessed PG output in <em>Pulsar Wg'lett</em> (2006).

Every table in the program looks like this and answers to the same six commands,
whether it holds a waveform lasting a few milliseconds or an amplitude contour
lasting a minute. That uniformity is not a saving in interface work. It is the
object-oriented paradigm surfacing in the design. Object orientation should be
seen "beyond its computational realm as an embodiment of a specific
compositional approach where concepts of encapsulation, inheritance and
polymorphism become operational as practical tools applied within the process of
composition" (Vaggione 1991, cited in Pietruszewski 2024, p. 54).

Polymorphism is the operative one here. Vaggione describes it as an arrangement
in which "the names of objects are handled in such a way that inside the common
network, objects receiving identical messages, when activating their specific
properties and methods, can on account of the latter produce uniquely different
results" (Vaggione 1991, p. 213). A table in these programs is exactly that. In
its basic form it is a list of 2048 values between 0 and 1, and nothing more.
Only after passing a graph object into a context, pulsaret or envelope
or sequencer, does the range of its values get updated to a new state, and how
those values are read changes with the context too (Pietruszewski 2024, p. 54). The `I` button makes the point plainly: it will take the first 2048
samples of the left channel of almost any sound file and put them in any table,
so a fragment of a recording can become a waveform, an envelope or a minute-long
frequency trajectory depending only on which window you pressed it in.

### New Pulsar Generator (nuPG)

The New Pulsar Generator (nuPG) is a reimplementation and extension of the Pulsar Generator developed by Marcin Pietruszewski (2021). Built in SuperCollider 3, nuPG preserves the core parametric architecture of the original while expanding the synthesis engine and interface.

The reimplementation retains the PG's 2048-point table structure and emission-rate quantization even though contemporary systems could support arbitrary table sizes and sub-sample precision. These constraints persist as conceptual commitments rather than technical necessities: to discard them would be to discard the conception of digital temporality that makes pulsar synthesis distinctive (Pietruszewski 2026). Where the original PG was a closed application whose synthesis logic sat behind a graphical interface, nuPG exposes that logic as readable, modifiable code, making the historical design decisions available for inspection through use. The design lineage connecting Xenakis's UPIC, the PulsarGenerator, and nuPG shows digital instruments carrying their design histories as active artifacts (Pietruszewski 2020b). The extensions carry it furthest from the original: a Fourier window, a masking window inherited from the PG, group offsets for phase-shifting the trains against one another, a modulation matrix routing four modulators to any parameter, a sieve editor that builds Xenakian sieves from logical combinations of residue classes and hands the result back as a table, and a neural synthesis network of sixteen units in four clusters whose activations become eleven further modulation sources. All of it terminates in the same place: a table of values read at a settable rate (Fig. 11).

The program is a class library rather than a patch. An application object boots a chosen number of trains across a chosen number of channels, and the state of every parameter lives in control values wired to the interface through the Connection quark, so a slider, a table, a modulator and a MIDI message all reach the same target by the same route. Alongside the synthesis classes sit analysis and corpus classes, an MLP regressor, spectral delay, flux and freeze, wavelet gating, and the neural table generator that writes tables rather than reading them. The synthesis engine itself is switchable at startup. `\classic` is the `GrainBuf` lineage the entry has been describing, one grain per trigger, the pulsaret read once per emission. `\oscos` replaces it with `PulsarOS`, an oversampling pulsar generator written by Jan-Lars Kleine-Tebbe and distributed in the <a href="https://github.com/dietcv/GrainUtils">GrainUtils</a> plugin, which takes separate buffers for pulsaret, envelope and modulator and reads each at an oversampled rate so that high formant settings do not fold aliases back down into the spectrum.

That mode also changes where a pulsar begins. `Impulse.ar` can only fire on a sample boundary, so at high emission rates the train is quantized to the sample grid and its period jitters. nuPG instead runs a phase ramp at the fundamental, detects the wrap, and recovers from the ramp's slope how far past the boundary the true trigger fell; that fraction is latched and passed to `PulsarOS` as a sub-sample offset, so each pulsaret starts at the moment the phase actually crossed rather than at the next sample. The emission clock stops being the sample rate.

That method is not nuPG's own, and it reaches well past pulsar synthesis. It
comes from a long line of work by Kleine-Tebbe, posting as dietcv, on deriving
events from phase ramps rather than from `Impulse`. The
<a href="https://scsynth.org/t/sub-sample-accurate-events-from-phasor-ramps/8856">thread
that works it out</a> (2024) builds the `rampToSlope` and `rampToTrig`
abstractions from Wakefield and Taylor's <em>Generating Sound &amp; Organizing
Time</em> (2022) in SuperCollider, and the
<a href="https://scsynth.org/t/grainutils-sub-sample-accurate-eventscheduler-and-dynamic-voiceallocator/12197">GrainUtils
thread</a> that followed runs to well over a hundred posts, working through an
event scheduler, a dynamic voice allocator that reuses free channels instead of
cycling through them, and the oversampled readers. Anyone granulating in
SuperCollider stands to gain from it, whether or not they ever emit a pulsar.
Read alongside the entry, it also shows how a technique gets maintained now:
not in a manual or a paper but in a forum thread, in public, over years. It is worth noticing what this does to the argument above: the Pulsar Generator's quantization of updates to the emission rate was a constraint of its time that hardened into a temporal commitment, and here that commitment is held while the quantization underneath it is dissolved.

### PdPG (Pure Data)

Both programs so far assume SuperCollider. <a href="https://github.com/whosebodyisthis/PdPG">PdPG</a>, by Ben Mason, is a
Pure Data implementation of the Pulsar Generator, released at v1.0.0 in October
2024 under GPL-3.0 and still described by its author as work in progress; it
needs Pd 0.54 or newer and the FluCoMa toolkit. Its synthesis strategy differs
from both SuperCollider versions. Rather than scheduling one grain per pulsar,
the train is computed continuously and gated whenever the phase runs past the
duty cycle. The released version does this in a single `fexpr~`, sample by
sample, with linear interpolation on the table reads written out by hand; newer
versions replace it with `tabread4~`, trading sample-wise accuracy for greater
efficiency with vanilla objects. Masking offers a choice
between Schillinger-style interference of three ratios and a stochastic
probability test. The table argument survives the port, and in one respect is
put more plainly: the nine meso-scale modulator tables, among them fundamental,
formant, amplitude and pan, are filled from the same folder of wavetable files
as the pulsarets, each read by its own `phasor~` at its own rate. A waveform and
a minute-long parameter trajectory are the same kind of file here, and nothing
but the speed of reading distinguishes them. The tables are audio files of any
length rather than a fixed 2048 points, which makes the choice visible as a
choice. The FluCoMa dependency is what makes the
pulsaret itself continuously variable: a `fluid.knnregressor`, from the
<a href="https://www.flucoma.org/">Fluid Corpus Manipulation</a> toolkit
(Tremblay, Roma and Green 2021), interpolates smoothly between four stored
pulsarets at control rate, selected by the per-train `net` switch. Where the PG crossfades between whole stored settings, PdPG moves
through the space between four tables and arrives at ones that were never stored
(Fig. 12).

## Music with pulsar synthesis

The astrophysical object has entered music by more than one route, and not always through the technique named after it. Gérard Grisey's *Le Noir de l'Étoile* (1989-90) for six percussionists predates all of the computer music here and synthesises nothing: it uses recordings of the pulsars Vela and 0359-54 detected by the Nançay radio telescope as integral elements, played back over twelve loudspeakers ringed around the audience, sounding in two sections at 22'07-24'53 and 35'53-37'59 against Les Percussions de Strasbourg. At the 1992 Brussels premiere the intention had been to stream the signal live from Nançay; it was not technically feasible, so a recording stood in. The work is nonetheless the clearest case of the question pulsar synthesis formalises, which is what happens to a periodicity when it is placed among others: Grisey sets the star's period against the players' and lets the two run.

Marcus Schmickler's *Bonner Durchmusterung* (2009) takes the numerical route. Made with the programmer Alberto de Campo and the astronomers Michael Geffert of Bonn University's Argelander Institute and Kerstin Jaunich of the Deutscher Musikrat, commissioned for the International Year of Astronomy and premiered at the Bundeskunsthalle in Bonn, it comprises ten compositions of three and a half minutes each, sonifying different sets of astrophysical data. One of them, *Pulsars/Neutron Stars*, takes sixteen rotation profiles and renders them with Tommi Keränen's PG (2004), mapping each profile onto "dynamic, fundamental frequency, formant frequency and spatialization". The arrangement lets several profiles sound at once, which Schmickler calls a form of "auditory comparability" (quoted in Pietruszewski 2024, p. 116).

Pietruszewski's *Synthetic Pulsar (Speculative Sonification)* (2021) runs the data through nuPG. The source is the European Pulsar Network database, the cataloguing undertaken by the Max Planck Institute for Radioastronomy at Effelsberg with seven partner institutions, whose raw sets "consist of a timing index and rotational profile of selected pulsars" and arrive as comma-separated values. Those profiles are not mapped onto sound directly but used as one term in what the accompanying paper calls "augmented sonification where an auditory stream becomes modulated by an additional process or data". The additional process is a model of attraction and repulsion "within a digital model of a world with its own size, dimensions and gravity". *Speculative sonification* is Pietruszewski's own coinage, and the word marks a double position: the pairing of measured rotation profiles with that model is "conjectural rather than relational in regard to observed and measured phenomena", and by sonifying "impossible" objects the work treats intuition and spatial experience as part of how knowledge is formed rather than as its decoration (Pietruszewski 2022). The work was commissioned by CTM Festival Berlin and Deutschlandradio Kultur and first presented as an installation on a purpose-built 64-channel system at Vollgutlager in Berlin, with the performance artist and vocalist Alex Freiheit as narrator and the synthetic-voice collective Birds on Mars; a binaural version was broadcast in Deutschlandradio Kultur's Klangkunst series. Jocelyn Bell Burnell, who discovered pulsars in 1967, served as scientific advisor. The loop closes on itself there: the person who first read a periodic signal off a chart recorder advising on a work that reads rotation profiles back out through a synthesis technique named after what she found.

The technique itself was developed inside a composition rather than before one. Roads
built pulsar synthesis while realising *Clang-Tint*, commissioned by the Japanese
Ministry of Culture and Kunitachi College of Music, Tokyo; its second movement,
*Organic* (1994), "combines bursts of insect noise and animal and bird calls with
electronic pulse-tones", and its asynchronous pulsar clouds were made by mixing
several infrasonic trains beating between 6 and 18 Hz (Roads
2001a). *Half-life* (1998-99) narrows the source to a single
gesture: "the raw material ... is a one-minute pulsar train that is wildly
varied", from which most of the rest of the work is derived by granulation,
microfiltration, pitch-shifting, recirculating echo, per-pulsar amplitude shaping
and selective reverberation. Its first movement is called *Sonal Atoms*.
*Tenth vortex* (2000) and *Eleventh vortex* (2001) continue in that direction,
the former built from a constant-Q granulation of a stream of synthetic pulsars.

The Pulsar Generator's recorded traces run in two directions. Marcus Schmickler
transformed PG material into the dense assemblages of
<a href="https://editionsmego.bandcamp.com/album/altars-of-science"><em>Altars of Science</em></a>
(Editions Mego, 2007); Florian Hecker presented raw, unprocessed PG output as
*Pulsar Wg'lett* on
<a href="http://florianhecker.blogspot.com/2006/12/release-recordings-for-rephlex.html"><em>Recordings for Rephlex</em></a>
(Rephlex, 2006). One treats the program as a source of material for later work,
which is what its designers expected; the other treats its unedited output as
finished, which they did not.

<a href="https://fancyyyyy.bandcamp.com/album/the-new-pulsar-generator-recordings-volume-1"><em>The New Pulsar Generator Recordings Volume 1</em></a>
(Fancyyyyy, 2020) collects fifteen pieces made with nuPG, four of them composed
at ZKM Karlsruhe in 2018, mastered by Russell Haswell and issued with a thirty-page
book, *Sound Composition with Pulsars*, written by Roads for the release.

Jung An Tagen and Eric Frye put nuPG to a purpose the technique's own literature
does not anticipate, and take their titles seriously. On
<a href="https://jungantagen.bandcamp.com/album/pulsar-acid"><em>Pulsar Acid</em></a>
(2020), twelve tracks make pulsar trains behave like a sequencer's bassline; on
<a href="https://superpang.bandcamp.com/album/phantom-acid"><em>Phantom Acid</em></a>
(Superpang, 2022), textures "from Marcin Pietruszewski's nuPG" are cut into
twenty-four tracks of exactly a minute and a half each, "laid out in enumerative
fashion". The grid is the form. A technique built to make the boundary between
rhythm and pitch continuous is here used to service a fixed metre, which is a
sharper test of it than any sympathetic application would be.

<a href="https://pwgen20.bandcamp.com/album/pulsar-scramble"><em>Pulsar.scramble</em></a>
(2020) and its
<a href="https://pwgen20.bandcamp.com/album/pulsar-scramble-vol-2">second</a> (2020)
and <a href="https://pwgen20.bandcamp.com/album/pulsar-scramble-vol-3-2">third</a>
(2021) volumes, released by $ pwgen 20, gather new work by more
than thirty other composers, most of it made with nuPG, among them Farmers
Manual, EVOL, Russell Haswell, Ben Vida, Lauren Sarah Hayes and Felisha Ledesma.
Three volumes over two years is a small corpus, but it is the point at which the
technique stops being one composer's method and becomes something a scene uses.
Victor Moragues's <a href="https://victormoragues.bandcamp.com/album/scan-pulsar-detours"><em>Scan Pulsar Detours</em></a>
came out of the same circle.

Jules Rawlinson has taken it in a direction of his own, across
<a href="https://pixelmechanics.bandcamp.com/album/pulsar-retcon"><em>Pulsar Retcon</em></a>
(Superpang SP63, 2021), *Pulsar Hexerei*, *Pulsar Threading* and a run of live
performances.
His method inverts the usual order. Rather than composing with the program, he
records improvisations from it and then treats the result as a corpus to be
played: "improvised buffer scratching, corpus scrubbing and waveform scuffing of
material sourced from New Pulsar Generator". The recordings are analysed with
the FluCoMa toolkit and navigated by descriptor with multitouch controllers, so
the instrument being performed is a sound bank rather than a synthesiser, and
pulsar synthesis becomes the thing that fills it.

The nine titles on *Pulsar Retcon* are worth a moment. *Lotus Prancer*,
*Protean Curls*, *Neutral Crops*, *Cursor Planet*, *Learnt Corpus*, *Nuclear
Strop*, *Tonal Sprucer*, *Near Sculptor*, *Opal Currents*: every one is an
anagram of *Pulsar Retcon*, the same twelve letters permuted nine ways. It is
the record's method stated in its own index. A fixed set of material, reordered,
is what the album does to the sound bank and what the technique does to a table,
and *retcon*, for retroactive continuity, names the operation exactly: going
back into something already made and reading it differently.

Kayon Choi's
<a href="https://kayonchoi.bandcamp.com/album/order-and-interstices"><em>Order and Interstices</em></a>
(Rope Editions, 2026) shows where that leads. Its opening track is built on
Xenakis's GENDYN; the rest begin from material made with "Marcin Pietruszewski's
'nuPG (New Pulsar Generator)'" and are then morphed, with Antoine Caillon's RAVE
among the credited tools. Pulsar synthesis appears there not as the subject of
the record but as one supplier of material among several, which is the ordinary
fate of a technique that has been absorbed.

The cells above run in the browser, where the language and the synthesis server
are compiled to WebAssembly and the page owns part of the session. They carry
things that mean nothing outside it. The `~wave` and `~envelope` buffers are
allocated by the page and filled by the pulsaret builder rather than by any cell,
and lines such as `("HB_TABLE " ++ name ++ " [" ++ points.join(",") ++
"]").postln` are a private protocol: the page watches its own post window for
those markers and uses them to plot the tables and move the playhead across
them. Pasted into a local SuperCollider they would post noise and then fail on
the buffers that were never allocated.

The file above is the same instrument with that scaffolding replaced by its
local equivalents, plus what the browser cannot offer. It boots and allocates
its own buffers, carries a catalogue of pulsaret waveforms and envelopes after
Roads's figures 4.11 and 4.12 that can be swapped under a sounding instrument,
plots the tables with `.plot`, opens a slider panel, and records to disk.

It also carries the one part of the technique the cells leave out: pulsar
masking, in the two forms the Pulsar Generator implements for a single train.
Burst masking alternates <em>b</em> emitted periods with <em>r</em> silent ones,
and stochastic masking throws a weighted coin once per period, its probability
held as a fifth control table alongside the four the cells use. Both delete
pulsarets rather than quieten them, so the train keeps the period it would have
had and loses emissions from it: at a 100 Hz fundamental a ratio of 1:7 leaves
one pulsaret every 80 ms, which is the subharmonic at <em>f</em>/(<em>b</em> +
<em>r</em>) described above.
